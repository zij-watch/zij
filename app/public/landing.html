What 26 years show

  1. 916 → 1,804

    Burning seen from space did not fall after the 2004 ban. It rose until 2018.

    A Nile Delta season averaged 916 in 2001 to 2003 and 1,804 in 2005 to 2011: +97% (band +62% to +137%). The rise had begun before the ban and only three seasons precede it, so this cannot show what the ban did.

  2. −53%

    Since 2018 the Delta's season is about half below 2005 to 2017.

    882 a season in 2018 to 2026 against 1,864: −53% (band −55% to −50%). It is not a steady low: 2023 came back to 1,706. 2026 is preliminary data.

  3. +23%

    Upper Egypt's sugarcane burning seen from space did not fall.

    225 a season (February to April) in 2018 to 2026 against 183 in 2005 to 2017: +23% (band +10% to +31%). The count is of places and days with fire, not of cane burned. 2026 is preliminary data.

Seasons are counted in hotspot-days (VIIRS-equivalent): places and days with fire, as the satellites saw them. The full tables, and what this cannot show.

chapter one

Two satellites, two records

NASA’s MODIS has looked for fires since 2000. VIIRS joined in 2012 with sharper eyes: its pixels are 375 metres across, where MODIS sees a kilometre.

Over the Nile Delta in autumn, VIIRS on Suomi NPP records 11 to 20 times more fire pixels than MODIS on Aqua in most years, and 42 times in 2018. The gap changes from year to year. Laid end to end, the two records would show a boom in burning in 2012 that never happened.

The same fires, counted by both. Small fires slip between the coarse pixels; the fine ones catch them. Fields and fires are drawn, not observed.

chapter two

One thread between them

We don’t multiply by a fixed number. In the years both satellites flew, we learn how one record maps onto the other, place by place, then test the fit on years it never saw.

The older record is then read on the newer scale, with its uncertainty shown, so a fire in 2004 and a fire in 2024 count the same.
